With the digitised outer-wall temperature prescribed, the tube model's response is controlled almost entirely by the bed-side film
coefficient, and the first finding of the study is that the heat-transfer chain printed in Part II of Xu and Froment
\cite{xu1989b} does not reproduce their own figure. Evaluated as printed, the De~Wasch--Froment wall coefficient with the
Yagi--Kunii dynamic terms gives $\alpha_i \approx$ 3100--3700 W m$^{-2}$ K$^{-1}$ at the particle Reynolds number of the case, and
the Leva--Grummer correlation gives 940--1580 W m$^{-2}$ K$^{-1}$; the value back-calculated from the digitised wall and gas
temperatures and the wall conduction is \alphaIxf{} W m$^{-2}$ K$^{-1}$ (interquartile range 313--363). Used as printed, the
correlations overpredict the gas temperature by 70--115 K and the conversion by 0.14--0.22 (Table~\ref{tab:xf}, first row). Both
dynamic terms are linear in $Re\,Pr$ and were fitted at Reynolds numbers one order of magnitude lower than those of an industrial
tube, so the extrapolation drives the effective radial conductivity to values that no measurement supports. A displaced decimal
in the printed coefficient of the effective conductivity would resolve the discrepancy, but we treat this as a hypothesis
\todo{check the coefficients against De Wasch and Froment (1972) and Froment and Bischoff before asserting a misprint}.

The Xu--Froment case is therefore used as a \emph{verification} of the tube model with a matched film coefficient rather than as a
validation of the heat-transfer correlation. With $\alpha_i = \alphaIxf{}$ W m$^{-2}$ K$^{-1}$ and Latham's inlet effectiveness
profile, the model reproduces the digitised profiles with an RMSE of \xfRMSET{} K in gas temperature, 16.7 K in inner-wall
temperature and \xfRMSEinc{} in the conversion increment along the tube (Fig.~\ref{fig:xf}, Table~\ref{tab:xf}); the residual
offset in absolute conversion of about 0.04 originates in the first half metre, where the digitised curve and the inlet rule
differ \todo{comment on the inlet-region offset}. This verifies kinetics, thermodynamics, the energy balance and the wall
conduction chain. The pressure RMSE of \SI{0.25}{bar} with the matched coefficient, against \SI{0.04}{bar} with the Leva--Grummer run,
reflects the different gas temperature and hence density along the tube rather than a change in the friction model
\todo{confirm this explanation of the pressure residual}.

The bed-side coefficient is validated instead on the plant data, where the furnace model supplies the outer-wall condition and
$f_{htg}$ is a free parameter. Fig.~\ref{fig:latham} and Tables~\ref{tab:latham}--\ref{tab:cal} summarise the calibration. The
adopted fit ($\alpha_{top}$ fixed) gives $F_{gt} = \calFgt{}$, $L_q = \calLq{}$ and $f_{htg} = \calFhtg{}$ with tight intervals, a
$\chi^2$ of \calChi{} on 24 degrees of freedom (reduced $\chi^2$ \calRedChi{}), and reproduces the outlet temperatures of all
three cases within 4 K, the lower wall temperatures within 1.1 K and the upper wall temperatures within 12.2 K. The four-parameter
fit lowers $\chi^2$ to 30.5 but is degenerate in $L_q$ and $\alpha_{top}$ (Table~\ref{tab:cal}); fixing $\alpha_{top}$ raises
$\chi^2$ by \SI{14}{\percent}, more than the \SI{10}{\percent} we set as the threshold for preferring the simpler model, so both
are reported and the three-parameter fit is adopted for all subsequent work because its parameters are identifiable
\todo{confirm the choice of the alpha-top-fixed configuration for the RQ work}. The heat-transfer factor of \calFhtg{} corresponds
to a bed-side coefficient of about \alphaIplant{} W m$^{-2}$ K$^{-1}$ at mid-height of the Plant A tube (four-parameter fit), i.e.\
between the two literature correlations and four times the value that reproduces the Xu--Froment figure; the range 300--3000
W m$^{-2}$ K$^{-1}$ is the epistemic span carried into the uncertainty analysis through $f_{htg}$.

Leave-one-case-out cross-validation (last two columns of Table~\ref{tab:latham}) shows that the held-out upper-wall temperature is
predicted within 5--18 K and the lower within 2 K. The upper-wall error is systematic: Plant C2, at \SI{71}{\percent} rate, is
predicted too hot at the upper elevation, and Plant A too cold. The upper peep hole lies in the flame zone, where a single
parabolic release profile with a load-independent flame length cannot follow the change in flame shape with firing rate. This is
the main structural limitation of the furnace model, and it is why the automated tests for the upper-wall error are recorded as
expected failures against the 4.5 K target derived from the stated measurement uncertainty. The consequence for the conclusions is
examined in Section~\ref{sec:rq4}.

The closed-loop maps (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}) show the structure of the operating space when the outlet temperature is held by the
firing controller. At constant outlet temperature, the hot-spot temperature rises with load because the duty per tube rises while
the furnace-gas temperature at the flame zone is fixed by the fuel-to-feed ratio, and it falls with steam-to-carbon ratio because
the endothermic load moves towards the tube inlet \todo{check this physical reading of panel (a)}. The life map (panel b) is the
image of the temperature map through the Larson--Miller relation and spans about two decades across the space. Hydrogen production
(panel c) increases with both load and steam-to-carbon ratio, the latter through the shift of equilibrium, at the cost of a higher
firing factor.

The Sobol analysis over all seven inputs (Fig.~\ref{fig:sobol}, Table~\ref{tab:sobol}) ranks the drivers of the hot-spot temperature:
the specific firing factor ($S_T = \SobolFiringTwo{}$), the steam-to-carbon ratio (\SobolSCTwo{}) and the excess air (\SobolEAtwo{})
together explain \SI{82}{\percent} of the variance; inlet temperature, catalyst activity and load contribute below 0.1 each, and
inlet pressure is negligible. Excess air matters because at fixed fuel it lowers the adiabatic flame temperature and raises the
flue-gas flow, both of which move heat release down the tube. The ranking for $\log_{10} t_r$ is the same except that the inlet
pressure enters with $S_T = \SobolPlogtr{}$ through the hoop stress, which is invisible in the temperature. First-order and
total-effect indices agree within their confidence intervals for every input, so interactions are weak over the operating space and
the influence of each variable can be read from one-at-a-time maps. The methane slip is governed by the firing factor
($S_T = \SobolFiringSlip{}$) and the load, as expected for an approach-to-equilibrium quantity.

The surrogates that make the following campaigns possible are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:surr} and Fig.~\ref{fig:parity}. The
Gaussian process is selected for seven of the eight targets, and the physics features improve the hot-spot temperature, outlet
temperature and slip. Test RMSE is \surrRMSETwo{} K for the hot-spot temperature and \surrRMSElogtr{} decades for $\log_{10} t_r$,
two orders of magnitude below the calibration residuals, at a speed-up of \speedup{} relative to the physics model. The CV+
intervals over-cover: at a nominal level of 0.90 the empirical coverage is 0.94--0.97 for all targets (\PICPtwo{} for the hot-spot
temperature). Over-coverage is expected for CV+, whose guarantee is one-sided, and is conservative; the interval widths are
nevertheless small enough (\SI{0.25}{K} for the hot-spot temperature) that surrogate error is immaterial to the conclusions.

\subsection{RQ2: life cost of flexible operation and ageing}
\label{sec:rq2}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig11_scenarios_summary}
\caption{Annual life consumption per kmol of hydrogen relative to the steady base S1 for the RQ2 scenarios, from hourly accumulation
(bars) and from a single evaluation at the annual-mean inputs (diamonds). Source: \texttt{data/scenarios/summary\_v2.csv}, \rdtversion.}
\label{fig:scenarios}
\end{figure}

\input{tables/tab_scenarios}

Table~\ref{tab:scen} and Fig.~\ref{fig:scenarios} answer RQ2 for one year of operation at the calibrated base. Under the
outlet-temperature controller, load-following costs almost nothing in tube life per unit of hydrogen: the daily profile (S2) and the
renewable-following profile (S3) consume \SxDaily{} and \SxRenew{} of the S1 life per kmol H$_2$, i.e.\ slightly \emph{less}, because
the hot-spot temperature at constant outlet temperature falls with load (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}a) and the hours at reduced load more
than compensate the hours at overload. Hydrogen output falls by \SI{10}{\percent} and \SI{15}{\percent} respectively, so the
absolute annual damage falls in proportion. A steady-state assessment at the annual-mean inputs reproduces the hourly accumulation
to within \SI{2}{\percent} for every scenario without discrete events (ratio \SxAvgErr{} for S3), which means that the non-linearity
of the Larson--Miller relation is not strong enough over the load range of this controller to make time-resolved simulation
necessary for smooth load variations \todo{discuss whether this conclusion holds for a plant without outlet-temperature control}.

Discrete over-firing events are different in kind. During a mild event (\SI{+10}{\percent} firing) the damage rate is \evMild{} times
the base rate, during a severe event (\SI{+25}{\percent}) it is \evSevere{} times, and a \SI{40}{K} hot band raises it by a factor of
\evHot{}; but because the events are short, their share of the annual damage is 0.4--2.7\,\% (\evHotShare{}\,\% for the hot band)
and the annual life cost per kmol rises by at most \SI{3}{\percent}. The annual-mean evaluation underestimates the event scenarios
(diamonds below bars in Fig.~\ref{fig:scenarios}), as it must for a convex damage law, but the error is small at these event
frequencies. Steam-to-carbon ratio has a larger steady effect: operating at 2.5 costs \SxSClow{} and at 3.5 costs \SxSChigh{} of the
S1 life per kmol H$_2$, because the higher ratio both cools the hot spot and raises the hydrogen yield.

Catalyst ageing dominates every operational effect. With the activity falling from 0.30 to 0.10 over four years and the controller
holding the methane slip, the life cost per kmol H$_2$ rises from \SxAgeSlipYone{} in the first year (fresh catalyst) to \SxAgeSlip{}
in the fourth as the hot spot climbs from \TwoAgeStart{} to \TwoAgeEnd{} K; holding the outlet temperature instead gives
\SxAgeTout{} in the fourth year because the slip is allowed to rise. Over the campaign the life consumed in year 4 is three times
that in year 1, and the question of when to change the catalyst is therefore also a tube-life question
\todo{comment on whether the activity range 0.30--0.10 is representative of a campaign}.

\label{sec:rq3}

\label{fig:pareto}

The Pareto set (Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}, Table~\ref{tab:regimes}) contains \nPareto{} members, of which \nFeasible{} remain feasible
under the physics model; the maximum discrepancy between surrogate and physics on the set is 0.03 in relative life and below
\SI{0.1}{MJ\,kmol^{-1}} in fuel. The calibrated base is dominated by \nDominating{} members. The knee of the front raises hydrogen
by \kneeHtwo{}\,\% while cutting fuel per kmol by \kneeFuel{}\,\% and life consumption per kmol to \kneeLife{} of the base; it does so
by running at \SI{110}{\percent} load with the minimum excess air, the maximum inlet temperature and a lower firing factor. At
constant production (iso-H$_2$ regime), the life cost can be reduced to \isoLife{} of the base with \isoFuel{}\,\% fuel by reducing
the firing and letting the hot spot fall from \baseTwo{} to \isoTwo{} K. The maximum-hydrogen corner (\maxHtwoGain{}\,\%) costs
\maxHtwoLife{} times the base life per kmol and runs close to the wall-temperature limit.

Every member of the front sits at the upper bound of the steam-to-carbon ratio (3.96--4.00). This is an artefact of the objective:
with firebox fuel alone, additional steam is free, and the equilibrium shift and the cooling of the hot spot both favour maximum
steam. The steam-credit sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:steam}, Table~\ref{tab:steam}) shows how the answer changes when steam raising and feed
preheat are charged. With steam fully charged ($\alpha = 0$) the minimum-fuel regime moves to a steam-to-carbon ratio of
\steamMinFuelZeroSC{} and the knee to 3.3, while the minimum-life regime stays at 4.0 but now costs \steamMinLifeZeroFuel{}\,\% more
total fuel than the base for a life cost of \steamMinLifeZero{}. For $\alpha \ge 0.5$, i.e.\ when at least half of the steam duty is
recovered from the flue gas or credited as export steam, the ratio of 4.0 is optimal for all three regimes. The plant's own steam
balance therefore decides whether tube life and fuel economy pull in the same direction or against each other
\todo{relate the steam-credit range to a typical SMR steam balance with waste-heat boiler and export steam}.

\label{sec:rq4}

Table~\ref{tab:uq} and Fig.~\ref{fig:uq} give the propagated uncertainty. At the base point the \SI{90}{\percent} interval of the
hot-spot temperature is \uqTwoWidth{} K wide and that of $\log_{10} t_r$ spans \uqLogtrLo{}--\uqLogtrHi{}, i.e.\ a factor of 50 in
absolute rupture time. The paired ratios are much tighter: the knee regime costs 0.20--0.64 of the base life per kmol and the
fourth ageing year 1.8--9.0 times, so the ranking of regimes survives an uncertainty that makes the absolute life meaningless. The
nominal result lies inside the \SI{90}{\percent} interval at every point. The variance decomposition (Table~\ref{tab:var},
Fig.~\ref{fig:variance}) attributes \shareCreep{}\,\% of the variance of $\log_{10} t_r$ to the creep group, dominated by the
heat-to-heat scatter and the Larson--Miller constant, 23\,\% to the kinetics, 9\,\% to the wall-temperature measurement and only
\shareHT{}\,\% to the calibrated heat-transfer parameters; for the hot-spot temperature the kinetics group dominates
(\shareKinTwo{}\,\%), followed by the measurement offset and the wall thickness. The one-at-a-time shares sum to within 0.04 of one,
and the Sobol total effects on the surrogate confirm the ranking, so the groups act almost additively.

The robustness fractions (Table~\ref{tab:robust}) state which conclusions hold across the joint uncertainty. That the fourth ageing
year costs more than twice the S1 life per kmol holds in \robAge{} of the samples, that the knee regime halves the life cost in
\robKnee{}, and that steam-to-carbon 3.5 is cheaper in life than 2.5 in \robSC{}; these three conclusions are robust and, for the knee,
become more so under the flame-length variant. The conclusion that flexible operation costs no life per kmol is not robust: it holds
in \robFlexNom{} of the samples with the calibrated flame length, close to a coin toss because the nominal effect is within
\SI{2}{\percent} of unity, and it is reversed in all samples when the flame shortens with load, under which the hot spot at
\SI{70}{\percent} load moves up the tube into the flame zone and the life cost per kmol rises to \lqvarLoadLow{} times the base. The
sign of the flexible-operation effect is therefore determined by a structural feature of the furnace, the response of the flame
length to load, that the three plant cases cannot identify.
